👴 Deep dive: The Grandparent Scam How it works, real examples, and how to protect your family →

The grandparent scam (also called the "emergency scam" or "grandchild in trouble" scam) targets elderly people with an urgent message: "Grandma, I'm in jail and need bail money." "I had a car accident and need you to wire money." The scammer pretends to be a grandchild, a lawyer, a doctor, or a police officer — anyone who sounds authoritative and urgent.

The scam works because it exploits love and fear. When a scared voice says "please don't tell Mom and Dad," the grandparent's instinct is to help immediately — not to verify.

How It Works

  1. The bait: An email or phone call from someone claiming to be your grandchild (or their lawyer/doctor).
  2. The emergency: "I'm in jail." "I was in a car accident." "I'm stuck overseas." "I need surgery."
  3. The urgency: "I need money right now." "Please don't tell Mom and Dad — they'll be so mad."
  4. The payment: Wire transfer, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or a "safe" courier.
  5. The hook: Once you send money, the scammer disappears — or calls back for more.

Real Examples

Email — Grandparent Scam

Subject: Urgent - I need your help

Hi Grandma, I'm so sorry to bother you but I'm in a really bad situation. I got arrested last night and I don't want Mom and Dad to find out. I need $3,500 for bail. Can you please wire it to me? I'll pay you back I promise. Please don't tell anyone. I'm so embarrassed. Love you.

Email — Lawyer Impersonation

Subject: Your grandson needs legal representation

Dear Mrs. Johnson, I am Attorney Robert Williams. Your grandson Tyler was involved in a serious incident last evening and has been detained. He has asked me to contact you for bail assistance. The amount is $4,200. Time is critical — please wire funds immediately to avoid overnight detention. I can provide my bar credentials upon request.

Email — Medical Emergency

Subject: Emergency - your grandson is in the hospital

Dear Parent, This is Dr. Sarah Chen at Metro General Hospital. Your grandson Michael was admitted last night with a serious injury. He is conscious but unable to make calls. He has requested that you handle the medical expenses. Please contact our billing department at [number] to arrange immediate payment of $6,800.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Urgency: "You must act now." "Time is running out." Real emergencies give you time to verify.
  • Secrecy: "Don't tell Mom and Dad." "Keep this between us." Scammers isolate their victims.
  • Unusual payment methods: Wire transfers, gift cards, cryptocurrency. No legitimate organization requests these.
  • Generic details: "Your grandson" instead of a name. Vague answers when you ask questions.
  • Emotional pressure: Crying, panic, begging. Scammers are actors — they play the emotion hard.
  • Bad grammar or odd phrasing: Many grandparent scam emails originate from non-native English speakers.
  • New email address: The message comes from an address you've never seen before.
How to Verify

Hang up. Call your grandchild directly on their real phone number. Call their parents. Call the hospital or police station directly — not the number in the email. Real emergencies don't prevent you from verifying.

How PhishDiver Catches It

PhishDiver's on-device AI and 15+ threat intelligence sources scan every email as it arrives. When a grandparent scam email hits your inbox, PhishDiver flags it with a red "Dangerous" banner and explains why:

  • Social engineering patterns: The AI detects urgency language, secrecy requests, and emotional manipulation.
  • Suspicious sender: The email comes from a free provider (gmail, yahoo, outlook) with no connection to your family.
  • Wire transfer requests: The body mentions wire transfers, gift cards, or cryptocurrency — classic payment methods for scams.
  • Threat feed confirmation: The sender domain or linked URLs are flagged by phishunt.io, PhishTank, or other feeds.

What to Do If You Receive a Grandparent Scam

  1. Don't click any links in the email — they may lead to credential-harvesting pages.
  2. Don't reply — even to say "this is a scam." Replying confirms your email is active.
  3. Verify independently — call your grandchild on their real number, or call their parents.
  4. Report it — forward the email to the FTC at spam@uce.gov and to reportfraud.ftc.gov.
  5. Delete the email — don't keep it in your inbox where you might accidentally click it later.
  6. Tell your family — warn your grandchildren and other family members that this scam is targeting you.
Protect Your Parents

The best defense is awareness. Install PhishDiver on your parents' or grandparents' browsers — it's free for Gmail and catches these scams automatically. Talk to your family about grandparent scams before they receive one.

FAQ

Questions, answered

Where does my email actually go?

Nowhere. Email content is analyzed entirely on your device by heuristics and the built-in AI model. The optional Gemini Nano engine, when available in Chrome, also runs on-device inside the browser. Nothing about your mail is ever uploaded for analysis.

What data does leave my browser at all?

Only narrow, keyless requests, and only when relevant: (1) URL or IP checks against public threat feeds — phishunt.io and PhishTank queries live; other blocklists (TweetFeed (URLs & IPs), Phishing.Database, Destroylist, IPsum, Blocklist Project, blocklist.de, CINS, FTC Do-Not-Call, and more) are downloaded on a schedule and matched entirely in your browser, so they never see your URLs; (2) public domain-registration (RDAP) lookups for domain age, from the registries' own public records; (3) a breach lookup to haveibeenpwned.com when you run the breach checker; and (4) a page fetch when you trigger a manual page check. Everything read is publicly available data — never email content, identity, or browsing history. See the Privacy Policy.

Which webmail providers are covered?

18+ providers: Gmail, Outlook (including the new Outlook), Yahoo, Proton, Fastmail, iCloud, Zoho, GMX, Web.de, AOL, Tutanota, Mailfence, Posteo, Yandex, Mail.ru, and more — plus a generic fallback for webmail PhishDiver hasn't met yet. Free covers Gmail; Pro covers every provider.

Which browsers are supported?

Any Chromium browser: Chrome, Brave, and Edge. The ONNX AI engine works everywhere. Gemini Nano adds a bonus layer only in Chrome 127+ where Google's on-device AI is enabled — on other browsers the built-in engine covers everything.

Is this a replacement for antivirus?

No. PhishDiver is an aid to your judgment, not a guarantee — and it shouldn't replace antivirus, a password manager, multi-factor authentication, or your own caution. Use it alongside those.

Why two AI engines?

Two independent checks are better than one. The built-in AI classifier is always on. When Chrome's own free Gemini Nano AI is available, it adds a second opinion on the intent and social-engineering behind a message. Both run on your computer — Google never receives your email. If Nano isn't available or you turn it off, the built-in engine keeps protecting you on its own, with nothing depending on an internet service.

What scam families does the on-device classifier detect?

The built-in ONNX classifier labels each message with one of seven scam families: credential theft (fake login pages), business email compromise (CEO/vendor payment fraud), gift-card scams, advance-fee scams (prize fees, prepayments, "approved" loans), romance scams, prize/giveaway scams, and sextortion/extortion scams (blackmail demanding Bitcoin). It runs entirely on your device in milliseconds, and the warning names the family so you understand what the email is trying to do.

Does it work offline?

Yes. The built-in AI engine, all heuristics, and downloaded blocklists work entirely offline — no internet connection needed for email analysis. The optional Gemini Nano helper also runs on your device, though Chrome may need to download that helper once over the internet before first use. Live threat-feed queries (phishunt.io, PhishTank) and domain-age lookups (RDAP) require an internet connection, but the extension degrades gracefully — if those fail, the cached blocklists and local AI still protect you. The core protection never depends on a cloud.

Will it slow down my browser?

Short answer: no for most people. The built-in AI model is lightweight and runs in your browser in negligible time — you will not notice it.

The Gemini Nano engine is heavier. On modern hardware (recent Intel/AMD laptops, Apple Silicon, discrete GPU), it runs with no visible delay. On older or low-end machines, it may cause a brief stutter. If that happens, you can turn Gemini Nano off in Settings — the built-in engine keeps running alone.

Battery: The built-in engine has a negligible battery footprint. Gemini Nano uses more power when active. On laptops, if you notice faster battery drain during long mail sessions, disabling Nano in Settings will help. Memory: The extension uses minimal memory for its core operation. Gemini Nano adds its own memory footprint managed by Chrome's built-in AI infrastructure.

Blocklist downloads happen periodically and are small text files. They are cached in memory only, re-fetched fresh on each browser start, and never written to disk. Background bandwidth usage is minimal.

What happens when I click a dangerous link?

PhishDiver uses two layers of protection. Known phishing domains are blocked at the network level before the page even starts loading — no flash, no partial load. For heuristic threats caught by the AI engine, the page is stopped on navigation and you get a warning screen that explains what the link really does in plain language — with Back to Safety, Allow Once, Always Allow, and Block options.

What is first-contact sender tracking?

When a message arrives from a sender you've never received mail from before, PhishDiver flags it with a visible chip. First-contact senders are the exact vector scammers rely on — you've never seen them before, so you have no baseline to compare against. The flag shows the sender's domain context and history so you can decide whether to trust the message, without blocking it outright.

What is Persona Guard?

Persona Guard catches attackers who impersonate your own contacts by display name. If "John Smith" suddenly writes from a domain you've never seen before — but John usually writes from Gmail — PhishDiver flags the mismatch. It's contact-impersonation detection that looks beyond the name to the actual sending domain.
